Course Description

Calendar Description for CS 889

View requirements for CS 889

This seminar-based course will be an examination of information visualization in scenarios of communication and collaboration between people: using visualization to inform, explain, persuade, entertain, reach a consensus, or some combination of these intents. Weekly student-led research seminars, and collaborative design exercises will prepare students with the necessary skills to undertake a substantial final project.

Audience

This course has no prerequisites. Given visualization’s multidisciplinary underpinnings (information design, human-computer interaction, computer graphics, statistics, databases, cognitive and organizational psychology), a variety of backgrounds and research interests are welcome. With respect to aptitudes that will be useful for this course:

  • Strong English communication skills (reading, speaking, and writing) are important for success in this course: there are substantial reading, writing, discussion, and presentation components in the required work. 

  • Prior coursework in human-computer interaction (e.g., CS 449/649) and user interfaces (e.g., CS 349) and / or prior industry experience with respect to information design and / or user interface {design | development | evaluation} will be helpful but not required. 

  • Students with expertise in a particular application domain ( e.g., bioinformatics, privacy, computer systems, etc) are welcome especially if they plan to incorporate information visualization into their thesis projects. 


This is predominantly a seminar-based course that does not teach visualization libraries / toolkits. Students are expected to learn and use appropriate libraries / toolkits as needed for their projects.

Learning Outcomes

No explicit learning outcomes defined for this course.

By the end of this course students should be able to...

  • Claim a foundational understanding of information visualization’s multidisciplinary underpinnings along with its theories and processes.

  • Be conversant in the topics, approaches, and methods of communication- and collaboration-themed visualization research.

  • Identify and reason about conventions in contemporary visualization practice, including deceptive and unethical visualization design choices.

  • Demonstrate a capacity to design, develop, and use visualization applications.

  • Articulate a critical position on visualization research papers and practitioner projects: summarize, interpolate between, and extrapolate from them.

Course Organization

The course is organized around 5 topics, with roughly two weeks devoted to each topic. The first two weeks will cover foundations in information visualization (InfoVis) and will alternate between instructor-delivered lecture, in-class group design activities, and discussion / critique. The remaining four topics include a traversal of the Communicative InfoVis design space, selected approaches for different communication modalities, selected approaches in collaborative visualization, and a foray into recent advances in immersive visualization. For each of these four topics, a typical meeting will begin with a brief instructor-led topic primer presentation that references optional readings, followed by an alternation between seminar presentations delivered by students, a discussion of the assigned content, brief group design and / or critique activities, and full-class discussions.

Each week, students are expected to complete the required preparation before each meeting. A typical week will involve reading and commenting on two-three academic articles (depending on the number of students enrolled, with one paper for each student seminar). Additionally, each assigned article will be paired with an artifact reflecting contemporary visualization practice, such as a web-based interactive article, a blog post, or a video, and students will be expected to interact with, read, or watch these artifacts. The academic articles will be a mix of foundational and recent papers, while the artifacts will be predominantly those produced within the last decade.

The course will culminate in a final project with several deliverables. 

The course will not provide instructions on how to use visualization applications or development toolkits / libraries in any great detail, but several recommendations appear on the resources page.

Class Participation

Attendance and participation during class and on Piazza are compulsory. 

Responses to Assigned Content

Each meeting of the class has assigned content. Beginning with the second meeting, students are expected to write a response to the assigned content, posting to the appropriate folder on the class’s Piazza instance (access code: 6xnl1cv7va3).

A response should be about as long as a typical HCI paper abstract (150 - 200 words). Responses are not summaries, as everyone is expected to have completed the assigned preparation. Since the assigned content includes both papers and other artifacts (e.g., interactive article, blog post, short video), a response should include the following:

  • Reflect: Comment on the contribution(s) of the assigned materials and their practical significance (1 - 2 sentences)

  • Interpolate: Associate or contrast the assigned materials assigned that week (1 - 2 sentences)

  • Extrapolate: Associate or contrast the assigned materials with the previously discussed course content and / or to works / papers beyond those in the syllabus (1 - 2 sentences… 3 - 4 sentences if extrapolating beyond the syllabus and links are expected to external content)

  • (Optional) Critique: Comment on perceived weaknesses or limitations in the assigned materials. 

The deadline for posting a response to the assigned content is 48 hours prior to each meeting. Overall, the responses are worth 20% of the total grade, with each (2%) marked on a pass / fail basis. Responses are informal (academic writing is not expected, but correct grammar and spelling is). Ultimately, student responses should be thoughtful, a demonstration that the assigned content was read and reflected on. Responses will be pass / fail graded, at least initially, if a large variation in quality is observed, quality based grading (2% for high quality, 1% for low quality) will be introduced.

In the 48 hours between the deadline and the meeting, students are expected to react and comment on others' responses (4% of total grade, pass / fail). 

The use of GenAI beyond typographical / grammatical correctness to write responses and comments is not permitted.

Student-Led Seminars

There will be at least two student-led seminars each week, assigned randomly by week 2. Each seminar will begin with a twelve-minute presentation of an assigned research paper*, following the format of IEEE VIS and similar conferences. These presentations should also be similar to the expected content of a conference presentation, including a summary of contributions, key takeaway details, methodological details, and implications for design; 12 minutes is not enough time to go into great detail with respect to prior literature and technical implementation details. Immediately after the presentation, there will be at most five minutes allotted so that classmates can ask the presenter clarifying questions about the assigned research paper.

Next, the seminar leader will coordinate a 20-minute discussion that draws from their classmates’ responses and replies to the assigned material posted 48 hours earlier on Piazza, split into the same three phases as the responses themselves: reflect, interpolate, and extrapolate. Seminar leaders must submit their presentation materials (e.g., slides, including discussion slides, if used) to the instructor within 48 hours following their seminar.

Aside from the seminar leader, another student will be the designated note-taker / scribe for each seminar, following a rotating schedule, randomly assigned by week 2. While the note-taker is welcome to engage in the discussion, their primary task will be to record contributions to the discussion (with speaker attribution). These notes can be typed or sketched, as long as they are added to the accumulated discussion notes for the course. 

Beyond the seminar leader and note taker, all other students are expected to contribute to the seminar discussion. 

Seminar leaders are not required to write responses on the week of their seminar. 

(*) Only the seminar leader has read this research paper, which is related thematically to the weekly topic and the assigned material that all students are expected to read. It will be therefore useful for seminar leaders to frame this paper as being in conversation with the assigned material.

Group Design / Critique Activities

Following the instructor’s primer and a student’s seminar (presentation + discussion), the remainder of each meeting, time-permitting, will be spent in small group activities, with group assignment following a permutation posted on the Schedule. No additional advance preparation is required for these activities. In the group activity of the week, the instructor will take a few minutes to outline the exercise or case study for critique that groups will undertake. At the end of the activity, each group will take a few minutes to share back their results / critique with the full class.

Course Project

Students are required to propose and present one research project. 

The general expectation is that this project will be conducted individually. Pairs of students are welcome to submit proposals, however, expectations for a project completed by a pair will be commensurately higher, and students will need to justify their choice to work as a pair in a project proposal, with a clear indication of students’ division of labour and expertise. 

Projects that align with those underway in other graduate seminar courses or with thesis / dissertation projects are also welcome. However, like in a project proposed by a pair, a proposal for a project aligned with other courses / thesis projects will need to clearly indicate prior or concurrent project work and how the course project will extend it.

Another general expectation is that students will undertake an implementation project involving visualization design and development, culminating in a { web | mobile | immersive } application, an interactive explorable explanation (in the style of distill.pub  or visxai.io), or a short “data video” documentary film. With instructor permission, students may instead opt to perform a literature review or tool comparison project. 

See Assessments & Activities below (Pitch, Proposal, Presentation, and Final Deliverables).

Project Pitch (3% - pass / fail)

By Feb at 13:00 (48 hours before the Feb 5 meeting), students will submit at least one project pitch (150 - 200 words and up to one image, such as a sketch, a slide, or a wireframe) to a dedicated Piazza discussion thread, outlining a rough idea of what you want to do, or what { data + task / communicative intent } you have in mind. The deadline will be 48 hours before the meeting. Between the deadline and the meeting, students are expected to react and comment on others' pitches. 

In the Feb 5 meeting, the instructor will collate the pitches into a randomly-ordered slide deck, and each student will be given ~4 minutes to elaborate on their pitch and invite feedback on it. This will also be an opportunity for those seeking to recruit project partners. 

Project Proposal (7% - pass / fail)

Students will refine their pitch into a proposal (between 500 and 1000 words + references)* and submit it on Tuesday Feb 24 by 13:00 (i.e., the return from Reading Week, 48 hours before the Feb 26 meeting).

In February, students will meet with the instructor by 15-minute appointment to discuss their proposal.

A proposal must include the following information and sections:

  • Student name(s)

  • Overview (1 paragraph)

    • The big picture context / domain for the project.

    • The project elevator pitch: one memorable sentence describing what the project will be.

    • The type of project (implementation, literature review, tool comparison)

    • Implementation projects must include the following:

      • (1) confirm availability (and appropriate abundance) of data;

      • (2) describe a domain situation or identify a known combination of task abstraction + data abstraction;

      • (3) a description of the intended audience / stakeholders for the project

    • (if applicable) relationship to other course projects / thesis / dissertation projects, and make sure to cite any already-published work

    • (if applicable) how the work will be divided

  • Related work

    • Aim for at least 10 references to properly contextualize your proposed approach and differentiate it from what has already been done

  • Proposed approach / method

    • This will vary by project type, but for implementation projects, it is expected that students will discuss your anticipated design and development process

  • Weekly milestones and outcomes

    • List the anticipated milestones and outcomes for each week until the end of term.

    • The only expected milestone and outcome after our final meeting are the final project deliverables

    • Expected outcomes are stated, including possible publication venues for the project

  • Bibliography

    • Use either ACM or IEEE citation style, but be consistent.

    • For each journal article entry, include title, full author list, journal name (and abbreviation, if applicable), volume #, issue #, DOI (or similarly stable link), and publication year*

    • For each conference paper entry, include title, full author list, conference name, (and abbreviation, if applicable), DOI (or similarly stable link), and conference year*

    • For pre-prints (e.g., arxiv), include title, full author list, year posted, and stable link

    • For books, include title, full author list, year published, and publisher

    • For book chapters, include full author list, chapter title, full book editor list, book title, year published, and publisher

    • For websites / miscellaneous, include name, URL, and date accessed

    • *… other bibliographic information is unnecessary (e.g., publisher, editor, page numbers, etc) 

Students must submit their project proposal via Microsoft Forms by 1pm on Feb 24 (48 hours before the Feb 26 meeting), submitted via this form.

Final Project Deliverables (25%)

A final report for an implementation project in the paper format must include the following sections:

Generative AI

Use of advanced AI systems to generate code (ie. CoPilot, ChatGPT, Claude etc.) is considered using code from other sources. Using GenAI systems in your project implementation is permitted as long as you disclose exactly what portions of your work were generated by AI. 

Using GenAI in project proposals and written final project deliverables is similarly allowed if you disclose which ideas, terms, sections, paragraphs, sentences, were generated by AI. The course will follow the University of Waterloo and ACM policies on GenAI.

In both cases, provide a record of your session with the AI system (the series of prompts you used and the answers provided by AI). This disclosure should be submitted with the course component. Failure to do so could result in a grade of 0 and/or academic integrity discipline.
