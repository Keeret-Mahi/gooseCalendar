Course Description

Calendar Description for SOCWK 120R

This course provides an overview of the social work profession and its place in the social welfare system in Canada. Students will examine Indigenous and Eurocentric histories, worldviews, values, and ethics while considering approaches to practice with individuals, families, and communities. Students will learn to think critically about various fields of social work practice as they impact marginalized and disadvantaged social groups.

View requirements for SOCWK 120R

Pedagogy

This course is developed under the premise that social work as a practice is learned and understood through both engagement in theory and active work in the field and that we must resist the colonialist limits of Western ontologies that delegitimize other ways of helping and healing. As such, the readings, activities, videos, and assignments that I have curated in this course will draw on these various ways of knowing and learning. Each week will combine a mix of reading, viewing/listening, discussion, case studies, and active engagement with the themes and practice spaces.

Your lived experience and interactions with other humans, non-human beings, and the earth are foundational to engaging with the course materials. This engagement could be rooted in the communities in which you have developed relationships (i.e. work, family, social organizations, activist groups, ethnocultural and/or religious communities, recreational/artistic/sporting communities, etc.). It might also develop through active engagement in social movements/groups that you have identified as being of particular interest to you in this course.

I would like to create a space that opens discussion between you and your classmates. This will be a place to grapple with the course materials in a respectful and thoughtful manner – it is not the same as Twitter/X or the comments section on social media – but rather a space for careful, humble, and serious discussion and debate. It should be a place where you are open to being challenged about your politics and positions, but also a space where your critiques should be tempered by your understanding that folks are entering this space with a desire to learn and change. This means that we should attempt to create a space of care, mutual respect, accountability, and trust – this is very difficult to do in any social space.

While this is a space of learning, it is also a space that seeks to resist the structures of oppression that permeate our day-to-day interactions. As such, I urge all students to be self-reflexive about behaviours or comments that have been identified as patriarchal, misogynist, classist, racist, homophobic, transphobic, ableist, et cetera. This self-reflection applies to me as well. We are all in a process of learning and unlearning these logics that dominate our society, so there is no expectation here that anyone is self-actualized and perfect – all I ask is that you are mindful of how what you think, say and/or the way in which you interact with others is circumscribed by these forces.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course students should be able to...
Acquire an understanding of the major concepts of the social work as a practice and as a profession
Be able to critically analyze the historical and current functions of the social work profession – including its carceral and liberatory lineages.
Gain an understanding and ability to both assess and apply social work values, principles, and theoretical models – but also to question their use in relation to practices of social justice
Can differentiate individual, family, group, and structural social work practice and understand their interconnections.
Developskillsandtechniquesthatwillbeusefulinavarietyofsocialworkfieldsincluding research, policy, grant writing, facilitation, group work, and individual counselling.

Challenge Levels Explained

I have assigned readings/media in this course that require different levels of effort and also come with different expectations of how deeply you engage with and understand the material.  If you think of the reading selections as a “workout regimen”, I’ve selected materials that work to “strengthen your knowledge” and are akin to working with a challenging weight. I’ve selected materials that help build your hope and excitement to “get your heart rate up” and are akin to cardio workouts. And finally, I’ve chosen some that you’ll need to sit with to really get, to still your mind or focus your analysis – think of these as “rest/stretching” type readings. 

I use the “challenge level” system here to give you a sense of how I would suggest preparing for the reading/media and what my expectation of you is after engaging with it:

Challenge Level: ¶¶¶¶¶

These are the most academically challenging of the readings.  You should feel a little bit frustrated by the language, a little confused or uncertain that you’ve grasped the argument, and perhaps a little bored/tired from the length and depth of the analysis.  That’s totally ok and part of the process of stretching your ability to critically think through important questions related to social work (and the world!).

My expectation: that you can relate the gist of the reading/media to a friend and family member in plain language but might not be able to fully get into the “weeds” of the analysis.  You should come out of the reading being confident that you understand the author’s central thesis.

 

Challenge Level: ¶¶¶¶

These are still academically challenging, but might be a littler shorter or might be “introduction” or “summary” type readings that do the work for you in providing you with the main thesis of the analysis.  I expect that these will feel both challenging and a little easier to “get” but might require you to focus/take notes to dig a little deeper than surface level.

My expectation: you should be able to explain the broad gist of the reading/media to a friend and family member  in plain language and perhaps to even talk more deeply with them about some of the finer points of the reading.  Nothing too intense, but at least some sense that you took something out of the reading.

 

Challenge Level: ¶¶¶ 

These tend to be readings/media that try to apply theory to practice. They usually have a lot of examples, case studies, and personal experiences peppered in with concepts and theories.  These readings are meant to challenge you to think about how what we learn in the course can be applied in the real world – but also the contradictions and tensions that arise between theory/practice. 

My expectation: these should be easier to read and maybe even more interesting (depending on your interests) than four or five star readings/media, but they are also pieces that might challenge you more emotionally and personally as they will delve into real world scenarios and get you to think about your own relationship to the themes/topics presented. My hope is that these types of works will be there to help you connect personally to what we’re learning in class.

 

 Challenge Level: ¶¶

These tend to be readings/media that are quite short but interesting and exciting to engage with.  They are often going to be online blogs, shorter readings, toolkits, and other types of content that is meant to give you some real-world prompts related to our course themes.  Perhaps they will be of use or interest to you as you move through this journey of life.  

My expectation: I hope that these types of readings/media offer you something useful to draw upon to actually see how the content we cover in class can apply to either your current/future work as a social worker or in your own personal life and relationships.

Assignment 1 (Solo): Social Work(ing) and You

Due Date: Monday, September 29th 11:59pm ET

Purpose

This assignment is constructed to align with Weeks 1-3 of the course content.  I’ve constructed this assignment to help you think more intentionally about what it means to practice social work, how your lived experiences inform what you think about social work, and to dig deeper into how we historicize social work and its role in society. It also gets you practicing self-reflexive thinking – or processing the content of the lectures and readings in relation to your own life and to your understanding of our current social and historical context.

What Social Work Skills Are We Practicing?

o   Understanding the purpose, history, and current reality of social work as a profession

o   Identifying practices of care and solidarity that are forms of social work not practiced by professionals

o   Honing your ability to think about the relationship between yourself and others

o   Identifying how identity, experience, history might shape the way we think about social work

o   Building our ability to differentiate between our aspirations, functions, and the contradictions that exist in the field of social work

 

Instructions

This assignment is broken up into three parts – each related to a separate week of the course. 

You are required to complete all three parts.

1.         Week 1 - We Keep Each Other Safe (min 200 words - max 250 words) - Describe a time in your life when someone or a group of people helped you address a problem.  How did they organize support? What resources were available or found to help? How did you feel receiving help? What expectations did those people have about how you would react/behave/respond to receiving this support?

You can draw upon concepts and knowledge from Week 1 readings and lecture content in your response.

2.         Week 2 - The Buffer Zone (min 200 words - max 250 words) – in Paul Kivel’s reading “Social Service or Social Change” he describes one of the functions of social workers as a “buffer zone”. In your own words, explain what he means by a buffer zone and provide two distinct examples for how social workers might function as a buffer zone. What might it look like to imagine social work as something other than a buffer zone?

You can draw upon concepts and knowledge from Week 2 readings and lecture content in your response.

3.         Week 3 - Personal and Professional (min 200 words - max 250 words) – Lori Campbell, the former head of the Waterloo Indigenous Student Centre, once recounted how social work university applications often prioritize and value volunteer experience but discount the work that people do (particularly Indigenous, Black, POC, disabled, neurodiverse, poor, queer/trans people etc.) to support their families, friends, loved ones in times of crisis or the experiences these communities might have with social workers (good or bad) and other institutions of care/control in our society.  Reflecting on the readings from Week 3 and your personal journey to taking this course, why might it be important to value lived experience in admissions to social work programs? If you don’t have a lot of lived experience, what work do you think you might need to do to gain skills and knowledge that will be helpful to people you’d like to work with?

You can draw upon concepts and knowledge from Week 3 readings and lecture content in your response.

Assignment 2 (Collective): Abolition & Decolonization

Due Date: Monday, October 27th 11:59pm ET

Purpose

This assignment is constructed to align with Weeks 4-6 of the course content.  The purpose of this assignment is to help you develop collective work skills and to explore what the concepts we are learning in our lectures and readings could look like in practice.  Influenced by grassroots social justice movements and in response to the increasing crisis of global authoritarianism - social work is undergoing a profound moment of change.

This assignment seeks to create a lower risk space to explore issues of settler colonialism, racism, and social transformation and to explore how we can engage in small scale actions to advance new concepts of care, accountability, and social transformation. It also gets you practicing collective-reflexive processes – or how you go about addressing power, complicity, conflict, and different work styles/lived experiences while trying to respond to important real-world political questions.

What Social Work Skills Are We Practicing?

o   Planning, coordinating, and engaging in collective work. Respecting the dignity and worth of all people.

o   Negotiating different work styles, capacities, skills, and finding ways to support each other and take accountability for your commitments while engaging in collective work.

o   Practicing forms of collective responses to questions of social and political importance.

o   Recognizing and honing our emotional labour skills – including practicing humility, accountability, and mutual aid.

o   Developing an understanding of the relationship between social work and social justice; and social work and truth & reconciliation.

Getting Started

During Week 3, you will be sorted into groups of 4-5 students – prior to the start of the collective work, you will exchange contact information, read the assignment instructions together, and take some time to brainstorm: (1) which of the two options your group should select; (2) think about the skills, interests, knowledge you might bring to the activity; (3) reflect on your capacity, work style, and what you might need to negotiate with others when working collectively .

Examples

o   I am someone who is prone to taking on too much – here are things I can do to share the load;

o   I am someone who struggles to meet deadlines – here are things I can do to try to communicate and reach out if I’m feeling like I can’t meet a deadline;

o   I am someone who struggles to get my points heard in group settings – here are things that I might need to ensure that I can fully participate;

o   I’m going through a tough time at the moment – here are things I might need to show up for this work while also taking care of my life stuff).

Instructions

Your Group will select 1 (one) of 2 (two) of the following projects to complete:

o   Option 1 – Truth & Reconciliation at Renison University College

o   Option 2 – Flip the Script: From Carceral to Abolitionist Social Work

 

Option 1 – Truth & Reconciliation at Renison University College

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada made 94 calls to action - while many of these calls were directed towards government bodies and other institutions, as we learned in the graphic essay, “Conversations in the Dark,” we all have responsibility for the work of reconciliation. 

For instance, Call to Action #63 focuses on education and reads: 

63. We call upon the Council of Ministers of Education, Canada to maintain an annual commitment to Aboriginal education issues, including:

i.                               Developing and implementing Kindergarten to Grade Twelve curriculum and learning resources on Aboriginal peoples in Canadian history, and the history and legacy of residential schools.

ii.                             Sharing information and best practices on teaching curriculum related to residential schools and Aboriginal history.

iii.                          Building student capacity for intercultural understanding, empathy, and mutual respect.

iv.                           Identifying teacher-training needs relating to the above.

Renison University College and the University of Waterloo, as educational institutions, and we (as faculty and students) have a responsibility and commitment to this work.

Your Group Task:  Your group is responsible for conducting a digital and spatial “truth gathering” exercise, followed by a reconciliation action plan: 

Truth Gathering

  1. Internet Research – Your group will conduct some internet research to learn about Robert Renison, the namesake of the college, and his affiliation with residential schools (in particular the Bishop Horden Residential School).

You should also try to find any information on how Renison University College tells its story and reflects on this past. Collect news stories, key information from website, and any other media that is helpful to your search.

  1. Walk Through - Your group will walk through Renison University taking note of signage, symbols, artwork, historical plaques that tell the college’s history and story.  You can document via photo, detailed notes, or video.  Be discerning in terms of what you pick up on – you should keep your eye out for items that affirm Canada’s settler colonial history and other items that disrupt it or acknowledge harm. 

Truth Assessment

Your group will produce either a 5-6 slide deck or a 5-6 minute video that documents the results of your truth gathering activities.  Be sure to respond to the following questions: 

a)         How is Renison University College implicated directly and indirectly in the residential school system?

b)         What does it mean to name the college after someone who was at the head of a residential school?

c)         How has Renison communicated this history to the public (online and within its physical spaces)?

Reconciliation Brainstorming

Your group will gather information on actions Renison has taken since the 2015 TRC was released to respond to the calls to action.

To do so, your group should investigate if Renison has a TRC plan on their website and document both its goals and the items it has completed.  Be sure to consider territorial acknowledgements, trainings, course content and programming, and any other items.

Think about what it means to address harm.  What are ways that Renison might do more to address the harms of residential schools, to be accountable for actively engaging in reconciliation activities, and to respond to calls for the school to change its name.  

Thinking alongside Frankie Noone’s “Conversations in the Dark” your group should also think about your individual and collective commitments to decolonization and reconciliation.

Reconciliation Action Plan

Your group will produce a 3-4 slide slide-deck or a 3-4 minute video that responds to the TRC calls to action. 

Your submission should offer concrete actions that can be taken by the following groups:

(a)      Students

(b)     Faculty

(c)      Administration and Leadership

Option 2 – Flip the Script – From Carceral to Abolitionist Social Work

As we learned in the Wong et al. and Preston readings, abolition is both a theory and practice. It emerges out of the long history of Black liberation struggles against enslavement, incarceration, and racial capitalism.  Women of Colour led movements like INCITE! and Critical Resistance helped to name and engage in abolitionist concepts within the areas of mutual aid, care, and harm reduction.  Modern social work is greatly indebted to the cultures of practice, failure, and re-imagining that emerge out of these movements.

In this option your collective work will be organized around responding to a real-world social work scenario that involves an individual seeking support from you as a frontline social worker.

Scenario

The Food Bank Intake Problem

The Job:  You are a frontline intake worker at a local food bank.  You are currently working on a part-time contract and your workplace is not unionized.  Your role at this job is to meet and greet people who are accessing the food bank for the first time and guide them through the intake process. 

The Intake Form: There is a standard form that the organization uses to process these applications, and your responsibility is to collect the data before the person can access food from the food bank.  The manager who trained you on using the form explained that the reason they collect this information is because the organization’s funding (including your contract) requires that you provide the funding body detailed demographic information about the population accessing the food bank. 

The form includes a variety of demographic information:  

o   the area that the person lives in the city (i.e. their postal code)

o   the racial/ethnic background

o   the person’s identified gender[1]

o   the person’s citizenship status

o   the size of their household

o   some basic questions about employment status. 

The Issue At Hand: Today as you were filling intake forms for people seeking to access the food bank for the first time, you came across a shy woman (about 40 years old) who came into the food bank with her young daughter.  The women only spoke Spanish and so her daughter (probably 8 years old) was there to help translate. 

The women was pretty stressed out and feeling nervous about accessing the food bank.  In the limited English she did speak, she kept repeating, “my family is hungry” to you.  You explain that she’ll have access to the food bank today and that you just need to fill out this form. The process was going fairly smoothly, as you navigated asking the questions, waiting for the daughter to translate, and then receiving the answer back through the daughter’s translation of her mother’s response.

When you got to the question about immigration status, the mother became visibly uncomfortable and upset and said “I don’t understand, my family is hungry.”  You explained that it’s just something you collect for the government to know who is accessing the food bank and once this is translated by the daughter the woman gets up and starts moving towards the door.  Wondering if there’s a miscommunication that might have occurred in the translation you explained that you’ll get your manager to help – perhaps she can smooth out the issue… but as soon as the daughter explained this to her mother, the woman grabbed her daughter’s hand and rushed out of the food bank.

Processing What Happened: This experience was really stressful and disconcerting for you. Something like this has never happened while you were doing intake.  You aren’t sure what to do and feel scared talking to your manager in case she thinks you’ve done something wrong. 

Instead, you talk to another co-worker who you’ve come to trust and explain the situation.  This co-worker suggests that perhaps the woman was undocumented, meaning she didn’t have legal immigration status in Canada, and she was scared that when you said you were getting the manager that you might be calling immigration enforcement.

The co-worker tells you they are part of a migrant justice group fighting for the rights of undocumented people to access city services without fear of deportation and explains that food banks aren’t allowed to turn away people based on their immigration status.  They tell you, in confidence, that this is a fight they’ve been having with the manager who says that she sympathizes with the issue but doesn’t want to jeopardize the organization’s funding by not asking demographic questions as per the grant requirements. 

The co-worker also says that when they do intake, they’ve long ago stopped asking for immigration status and just randomly click “Canadian Citizen” or “Permanent Resident” on the form because they don’t want anyone to be scared to access the food bank. They said they are willing to accept the consequences if they get caught, and that they’d consider calling out the organization on social media for creating an environment that makes undocumented people feel unsafe to access food that they need to survive.

You reach out to a group of friends that you met in your SOCWK 120R course and ask them to help you think through the incident and create a plan of action.

Your Group Task:  Your group is responsible for conducting a “carceral social work assessment”, followed by an “abolitionist social work action plan”:

Carceral Social Work Information Gathering

  1. Assessing the Situation – Your group will draw upon the readings from week 6 and the course content, to do the following:

o    Identify the core need being expressed by the person seeking support

o   Identify any organizational policies and practices discussed in the scenario and try to assess whether they might help or limit how you meet the core need of the person seeking support

Abolitionist Brainstorming

Drawing inspiration from Mariame Kaba’s “Abolitionist Imagination Cards” your group will brainstorm possible actions that could take place within, against, and beyond the current policy structure for your food bank when it comes to serving the undocumented population of the city. 

What does that mean?

Actions Within the System – might include strategies for limiting harm (i.e. see Sparrow Preston’s article “Abolitionist Disjuncture, Part II” pp.147-150 as one example); going through the appropriate channels to change policy; or evading the policy in similar ways to your colleague who just randomly marks the forms.

Actions Against the System – these are mid-range actions that try to prevent harm from happening more broadly. This could include getting involved or starting a campaign to prevent the asking of immigration information in food banks; getting involved with organizations who support undocumented migrants to help them fight for status; participate in political organizing that tries to shift government policy with respect to undocumented migrants (see No One Is Illegal as an example of one such group).

Actions Beyond the System – these are ideas for what food justice might look like that isn’t simply tied to food banks.  What would it look like to make sure that folks are fed in our community without fear of criminalization? Look up an organization like Food Share Toronto for inspiration (@foodshareto)

Your group will produce a 4-45slide slide-deck or a 4-5 minute video that responds to the lays out your proposed actions within, against, and beyond the system.  You should consider both the possibilities and risks of each proposed action.

Rejecting Late Marks as a Good Education Tool

We live in a world ruled by capitalist logics. This course rejects the role of academia in creating a docile and subservient working class.  That doesn’t mean that we are creating a system free of responsibilities, instead, I’m asking you to be both relational and accountable to me with regards to your educational needs and capacities. I will in turn work to be relational and accountable to you in terms of getting your grades back on time, responding to emails, and asking for more time if I need it too!

We’ve been living through heavy times. So many of us are going to school and juggling full-time work, childcare, parent care, and various mental and physical health issues.  We’ve been struggling to make time for joy and social events, for organizing in community, and taking time for rest – those are all important parts of our lives and we can’t relinquish them because we are in school.

I don’t believe lateness means a lack of effort, laziness, or inability to manage one’s time properly – those are myths perpetrated to create the uber productive pressure we all suffer from. I tend to believe that the best learning outcomes are ones where students feel supported and have a structure and relationship with the course and instructor that is healthy and accountable. I have created deadlines as a means of facilitating your work over the term, but I can adjust them for folks within reason – to better facilitate your learning. For that reason, I ask that you communicate your needs with me, and I’ll try to be open and up front about my needs/capacities with you and the class in general. We’ll weave through this together.

If you require assistance, contact AAS by phone (519-888-4567 ext. 35082), email (access@uwaterloo.ca) or in-person (Needles Hall North, 1st Floor, Room 1401). 

Turnitin.com: Text matching software (Turnitin®) may be used to screen assignments in this course. Turnitin® is used to verify that all materials and sources in assignments are documented. Students' submissions are stored on a U.S. server, therefore students must be given an alternative (e.g., scaffolded assignment or annotated bibliography), if they are concerned about their privacy and/or security. Students will be given due notice, in the first week of the term and/or at the time assignment details are provided, about arrangements and alternatives for the use of Turnitin in this course.

It is the responsibility of the student to notify the instructor if they, in the first week of term or at the time assignment details are provided, wish to submit alternate assignment.
