Course Description

Calendar Description for ERS 415

Continuing from concepts developed in ERS 215 and ERS 315, this course will focus on the latest concepts and applications of assessment principles and practices. The course focus will vary from year to year following development in the field. Topics may include assessment into land use planning and community design, policy and program assessment, and assessment of new technologies and alternative futures.

Roles and purposes of the course

As the final core course in the series for a Diploma in Environmental Assessment, ERS 415 is the culmination of a series of studies in the field. The course reviews and synthesizes material from the preceding pre-requisite courses on assessment principles and methodologies. The focus, however, is on the major current directions and trends in environmental and sustainability assessment – the most advanced thinking and applications in the field and the most pressing stresses today, especially in Canada. In particular, the course examines how new appreciation of complexities and uncertainties, and rising expectations for progress towards sustainability, are affecting assessment thinking and application in the face of the usual competing pressures for streamlined decision making and quick approvals for economically and/or politically attractive undertakings.

While assessment theory, law and practice have improved over the past 50 years, with greater practitioner experience and higher public expectations, they have also proved to have serious limitations. In Canada and elsewhere, most assessment processes have focused on individual proposed projects and have aimed to identify and mitigate their significant adverse effects. Some jurisdictions give serious consideration only to predicted negative effects on the biophysical environment. We have learned, however, that the most serious concerns arise from the combined social and biophysical effects of multiple undertakings, and public expectations are increasingly for projects to deliver positive contributions to lasting wellbeing rather than merely avoid serious negative effects. 

Consequently, assessments have been pushed to be more comprehensive (covering the complex interactions of cumulative as well as individual social and biophysical effects), more ambitious (aiming for positive contributions to sustainability as well as mitigation serious adverse effects), more far-sighted (especially where climate changes issues are raised), more broadly applied (to strategic level undertakings as well as individual projects), more transparent and credible (including more open and responsive to public engagement and more forthright about the reasoning behind recommendations and decisions). Also, assessment in Canada has been affected by expanding understanding of complex socio-ecological systems, greater recognition of Indigenous rights, and evident tensions about the distribution of benefits and risks (e.g., from transboundary hydrocarbon pipelines). 

The available approaches to addressing all these considerations should make assessments considerably better, but also more difficult, maybe more expensive and possibly slower as well. A major question for the future therefore is how to design and apply assessments more creatively so they can meet rising needs and expectations while also being manageable, affordable and timely.

In particular, project-level assessments have tended to be too narrow in scope and too late in decision making to address overall concerns about the integrity of ecosystems and the wellbeing of communities or to introduce significantly more sensitive and sustainable approaches. Moreover, conventional project-based assessments have tended to be inadequate means of ensuring properly integrated consideration of the interrelated ecological, social and economic factors that determine long term effects. 

The course will examine the nature, significance and application of broader approaches to assessment, and accompanying efficiency issues, with emphasis on Canadian cases in various jurisdictions, within and beyond the usual realm of assessment law. It will review the academic and professional literature on advanced assessment thinking and will emphasize critical examination of practical cases where advanced assessment initiatives have been proposed and/or undertaken. Participants in the course will be expected to become familiar with the main components of advanced assessment (now being explored as a “next generation assessment” package), to see how they may be integrated for application in actual cases and in the redesign of assessment law, policy and practical implementation. 

For particular examples, the course will often take advantage of the federal impact assessment law and process, which came into effect in August 2019.  While the federal process applies only to matters under federal jurisdiction, many assessment cases and issues involve the overlapping interests and responsibilities of two or more jurisdictions (federal, provincial, Indigenous, territorial, even municipal). As a result, the scope of the federal law’s implications extends across the country. 

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course students should be able to:
Apply understanding of the nature and implications of the core purposes of sustainability-based assessment as a tool for encouraging positive contributions to lasting wellbeing.
Recognize the main components of advanced “next generation assessment,” how they should interact and how they may be integrated for application in better planning and implementation of strategic and project level undertakings and in the redesign of assessment law, policy and practice.
Recognize the diversity of perspectives and immediate concerns among the authorities, stakeholders, experts and other assessment participants and the openings for approaches that encourage mutual learning and commitment to lasting contributions to the public interest.
Recognize the strengths and limitations of existing and proposed approaches to assessment, and build positive responses to associated opportunities and challenges.
Apply learning on the matters above with sensitivity to context in different cases and contexts, including in professional writing and public engagement.

The learning outcomes set out above represent more specific contributions to the following learning outcome objectives, which have been adopted for the undergraduate program of the School of Environment, Resources and Sustainability (SERS). These objectives are well suited to the interests of all ERS 415 participants, regardless of their academic homes.

  • Outcome 1: Understand the challenges and opportunities of moving towards sustainability.
  • Outcome 2: Define and articulate core skills and areas of knowledge relating to SERS’ areas of strength.
  • Outcome 3: Understand and work within, between and across disciplines and ways of knowing.
  • Outcome 4: Mobilize their skills and knowledge to contribute to better understandings, diagnoses and solutions for environmental and sustainability challenges and opportunities.
  • Outcome 5: Work collaboratively and effectively with people and organizations affected by or responsible for addressing environmental and sustainability challenges and opportunities.
  • Outcome 6: Communicate ideas effectively in different oral and written formats to professional and public audiences.

The questions and their implications will be considered from the perspectives of various interests.

The detailed lecture schedule is set out below. The first three sessions provide an overview and background to the main issues now facing assessment in Canada and some big issue cases to which we will be referring throughout the course. Panel presentations and class engagement

After the lecture discussion (roughly one hour), the remainder of each Wednesday meeting will be divided into two periods of about 45 minutes each. The first period will be devoted to considering the first class meeting discussion question for the session. The second period will address the second discussion question for the session. See part 2 of the syllabus on the details for the course sessions, including the issues, readings, and class meeting discussion questions for each session. See also the document on “415w24 Wednesday meeting timings” document in the syllabus file.

In most sessions, the questions will be addressed by participant panels and responses from class groups representing various interests. The discussions will consider implications for the design of next generation assessment law and policy regimes, in Canada and beyond, and implications for practical application in current or anticipated cases. As well, the discussions should be useful in preparing for the three briefing papers.

To facilitate all this, the class has been divided into eight groups (A-H) of five or six members each. Each group has been given rotating assignments through sessions 2-11. The assignments involve representing the (enlightened) understandings and interests of authorities, stakeholders or other groups that are major players in assessments and/or likely to be significantly affected by assessment decisions. 

The nine categories of authorities, stakeholders, etc., to be represented are as follows:

  1. public sector (federal, provincial, territorial) proponents of major projects or other undertakings subject to assessment requirements
  2. private sector proponents of projects subject to assessment requirements
  3. federal, provincial and/or territorial assessment agencies, reviewers and decision makers (includes civil servants and ministers and their political staff)
  4. Indigenous governments as responsible and affected authorities
  5. municipal and regional governments, local business groups and community organizations potentially affected by assessed undertakings
  6. non-government organizations focusing on ecological and social justice issues
  7. non-government organizations focusing on economic concerns
  8. independent experts on major assessment issues (e.g., ecologists, socio-economic analysts, climate change policy experts, Indigenous law specialists)
  9. advocates for the interests of future generations, including experts in the design of sustainability-based next generation assessment regimes (laws and policies, institutions, applications and practices)

The assignments for each session are set out in the “415w24 engagement assignments” document, which is in the Syllabus file under the Content tab on the course Learn site. 

Each team will serve as the panel members for one of sessions 4-11. Every session will have two panels (each made up of half the team) – one for each of the two discussion questions for the session. Each panelist will address the assigned question from the perspective of one of nine categories of main assessment authorities, stakeholders or interests set out above.

For the sessions in which they are not the panelists, each group has been assigned to think from the perspective of an interest from one of the nine categories. The assigned roles for the groups change from one session to the next. In their role for the particular session, the group members will develop their response to the panel presentations on each of the two questions. For each question in sessions 2-12, one individual in each group has been pre-assigned to be the group’s respondent (identified as “R” in the “415w24 engagement assignments” document on the course website).

The panel presentations and submitted scripts with references

For each of sessions 4-11, the discussion portion will address the two questions set out in the syllabus for that session. Consideration of each question will be initiated by a panel drawn from one of the groups (group A for session 4, group B for session 5, and so on). 

Each panelist assigned to the question will make a maximum two-and-a-half-minute statement setting out what are, in the panelist’s view, the most important matters and how they should be addressed as the answer to the question from the enlightened perspective of the interest(s) the panelist is representing. 

Each panelist will choose to represent the interests of one (or a combination of two) of the nine categories of assessment participants (authorities, stakeholders, etc.) listed on page 6 above.

At the session prior to the session of your group’s panel presentations, each panelist will meet with the instructor or teaching assistant in the first break to choose a category (or pair of categories) to represent. 

The panel presentations and the associated notes (script with references to the sources used) will be graded and represent 10% of the final grade. After the class session but before midnight that day, the panelists are to submit their presentation notes in the Learn dropbox for panel presentations. Feedback and grades will be provided through the dropbox. 

The panelists will be expected play their representation roles professionally and realistically. Their positions should draw from the readings and from material discussed in previous sessions (and previous courses) as well as from general knowledge of the key concerns of the interests being represented. The positions presented should be well-informed and enlightened as well as concise.

Panel presentations evaluation rubric

The central considerations for grading the presentations (and scripts/references) are as follows:

  • clear and constructive response to the question being addressed;
  • evident understanding of the concepts, issues and response options involved, and insight into their practical implications;
  • evident familiarity with the readings and other source material (references);
  • plausible presentation of the perspective of the enlightened players being represented, while also recognizing needs to find some measure of consensus (e.g., through points that might win support from participants with other perspectives);
  • synthesis, integration and drawing connections between and among the immediate subject matter and ideas, issues and insights from the course materials or elsewhere;
  • reference to at least one case example;
  • effective oral communication skills (clear, concise, credible, and able to keep within the time limit);
  • well-prepared and referenced script (or script notes) for the presentation;
  • accuracy and creativity in illustrating applications and implications (e.g., case examples) as appropriate; and as always
  • special credit for wit and humour.

Class engagement in response to the panel presentations

Except in sessions when they are serving as panelists, the group members will serve as commentators with assigned perspectives. After the discussion of a question has been introduced by the instructor and teaching assistant (sessions 2, 3 and 12) or by panelists from the assigned group (sessions 4-11), we will have a short (8 minutes) response preparation period for members of each group to discuss the questions and presentations and to prepare a group response to the question and the panel views. 

For most of the panels and questions in sessions 2-12, one group member from each group has been assigned to be that group’s respondent (see the “415w24 engagement assignments” document). The respondent delivers to the whole class a brief oral summary of the group’s response to the relevant question and panel presentations (about one minute will be sufficient). 

Each participant in the course has been assigned the respondent role for three sessions. Because respondents have not been assigned for all sessions, additional opportunities are available. If any of your assigned dates are problematic, you can trade dates with one of your group members.

As time permits, at the end of the discussion of each question, we will have an open-to-all opportunity to consider what level of agreement is evident or could be possible among the different (but enlightened) interests. However, we will also recognize areas of deep disagreement and consider the implications.

The “415w24 engagement assignments” document posted with the syllabus on the course UW Learn site is crucial and will be reviewed at the first class. It sets out the assignment of individuals to teams, to panel presentation sessions and questions, and to presenting group responses to the panels and questions. It also covers the assignment of the groups to rotating interest perspectives for sessions 2-11 (excepting when they are panelists). 

Individual contributions

While the course involves group activities and expects demonstration of collaborative skills, evaluations are based on individual contributions. As noted above, one session prior to their presentation session, participants in each panel are to meet with the instructor or a teaching assistant during the first class break to determine what categories of assessment participants they choose to represent on their panel. Beyond that, there is no expectation for the panel members or commentators to meet with their groups before the class to coordinate positions. The contributions of the panelists and commenting participants will be graded individually.

After each panel presentation, there will be a brief (8 minute) period during which each group discusses how to respond to the relevant session question and the panelists’ expressed views.  While the assigned respondent should already have prepared basic notes for a response, the other group members are to contribute ideas and suggestions concerning key points concerning the question and the views expressed by the panelists to help their respondent finalize the key points for the group’s response. Individual contributions include participation in helping the group’s respondent to finalize key points to present.

Recognizing the constraints of a large class, each participant is also encouraged and expected to engage thoughtfully in the open class discussions about the lecture as well as the session questions.

Evaluation of engagement will be based on the quality as well as the extent of contributions. Evaluation of engagement quality will take the following criteria into account:

  • evident understanding of the concepts and issues introduced in the session involved, in the context of the concepts and issues raised in earlier sessions, and insight into the practical implications of these concepts and issues;
  • evident familiarity with the readings and other source material;
  • careful listening and thoughtful reflection before making comments (especially in responses to the panelists’ points);
  • plausible presentation of the perspective of the players being represented (especially in responses to the panelists’ points) plus recognition of and respect for the positions of others;
  • synthesis, integration and drawing connections between and among the immediate subject matter and ideas, issues and insights from the course materials or elsewhere;
  • accuracy and creativity in illustrating applications and implications (e.g., case examples) as appropriate; and

  • special credit for wit and humour.

Each participant’s class engagement (other than the panel presentation, which is graded separately) will be evaluated in two parts (sessions 1-6 and sessions 7-12), each worth 10% – combined the class engagement grade will represent 20% of the overall course grade.  

The three briefing notes

Submission of three papers is required. All are to be in the form of briefing notes (also often called briefing papers) to relevant individuals or organizations. These writing assignments have two purposes. The main purpose is to encourage integration of understanding gained from the readings, lectures and discussions, with particular attention to the implications of what you are learning for practical application. The second purpose is to provide experience in a style of writing you are likely to use as a professional. 

Briefing notes are concise and carefully structured documents prepared for senior officials or the equivalent. The readers are assumed to have a significant need to know but little time to slog through pages of text to get to some conclusion at the end. Many different particular formats are used, but they all focus on getting the essential information across as quickly as possible. 

Usually, the key conclusions or take away messages go in a summary at the beginning. They are followed by crucial background, the main points of issue, identification and assessment of response options, and recommendations. The main body of the note rarely exceeds two pages and is designed to be easily scanned (informative subheadings, highlighted sentences, text boxes for key points). Supporting evidence and arguments, examples, references, etc., are often included in appendices. Even short briefing notes, such as your first briefing note, benefit from putting the details and background in short appendices.

Generic briefing note information from Rob Parkinson is available at http://writingforresults.net/. A guide prepared by Robert Fonberg is at https://uwaterloo.ca/defence-security-foresight-group/sites/ca.defence-security-foresight-group/files/uploads/files/dsfg_policy_brief_writing_guide_v2.pdf

Some examples of real briefing notes (mostly without the appendices) are posted on the course UW Learn site. The examples do not all use a standard format, and you can diverge from any proposed standard too, if you think an adjusted approach will work better for the purposes of your assignments. But basic principles still apply. Remember that real officials will rarely have time to read (skim) more than two pages – details go into easily skimmed appendices.    

Given the complexities involved (many different applications, players, issues, possible responses, etc.), you cannot discuss everything. In choosing what to include in the briefing notes, give particular attention to what you consider to be most significant for improving sustainability-based, next generation assessment law, policy and practice. You will have to consider carefully what is and is not crucial here.

Be clear and concise. These papers are meant to communicate essentials to very busy readers. They need key information in a format that facilitates a quick grasp of the material. However, they also need necessary clarifications and evidence (or references to evidence) that support the argument and demonstrate credibility and reliability. Remember that you are, at least implicitly, making an argument. Remember also that these are scholarly papers, expected to meet the usual expectations for sound argument, proper references and reasonable adherence to the conventions of grammar, even if you choose to rely to some extent on bulleted lists of major points. 

Late penalties will be assessed for briefing papers received after the due dates set out in the detailed instructions below for each paper. The standard penalty is 0.5 per day for the first three days and 0.25 per day thereafter (15/20 one day late becomes 14.5/20).

General rubric for grading the briefing notes:

Recognizing that each of the three briefing notes involves specific expectations that are set out in detail below, evaluation of the briefing notes will take the following criteria into account:

  • demonstrated understanding of the course material (readings, lectures and discussions), including evident familiarity (though not necessarily agreement) with the key concepts and issues, appropriate responses or response options and their interrelations, and attention to implications for applications;
  • clarity and coherence of response to the particular assigned task for the briefing note, including provision of a credible and persuasive argument, supported by appropriate evidence and reasoning;
  • attention to the perspectives of different interests and to the practical consequences (directly and indirectly) of your arguments and conclusions/recommendations;
  • effectiveness of presentation, including conciseness and readability in a professional approach to writing, using (with flexibility) a briefing note structure and style, and characterized by easily comprehensible layout and text;
  • suitable use of reliable sources and proper bibliographic referencing of sources used, including the lectures; and
  • special credit for wit and humour.

Briefing note #1: on sustainability-based next generation assessment

The first briefing note is a short warm-up assignment to (re)familiarize you with the briefing note style outlined above and to renew your understanding of assessment issues and options from the previous courses. The submission is to be a maximum of 1400 words, including the appendix but not including references. It is to be submitted electronically to the relevant dropbox on the course Learn website before midnight on Monday, February 5. This assignment represents 15% of the final grade.

Purpose: Briefing note #1 is to set out the main strengths and challenges of sustainability-based next generation assessment and offer recommendations concerning whether and how to adopt the package jurisdictions internationally.

Expectations: The briefing note must

  • cover the issues and materials addressed in sessions 1-3 plus the core insights from session 4 on sustainability assessment;
  • be addressed to participants in a workshop at the International Association for Impact Assessment (IAIA) conference in Dublin in April 2024;
  • aim to prepare those participants for discussing the nature and general desirability the next generation assessment package, and determining whether and, if so, how the IAIA should promote its adoption in jurisdictions around the world;
  • use Canadian examples of legislated provisions and practical experience to explain or illustrate your points and their implications to an international audience, and in doing so
    • ensure at least some of the law examples refer to the current federal Impact Assessment Act, and
    • ensure at least some of the practical examples involve the issues raised by climate change, extractive industry projects and/or the often regional scale of impacts and/or options (as illustrated by the big issues discussed in session 3)
  • provide general recommendations for consideration by the workshop members;
  • use the briefing note style outlined above;
  • ensure that the appendix material, like the main body of the note, is designed to be easily scanned; and
  • put the 14 next gen assessment components in a list or table in an appendix.

In drafting in the briefing note, you should

  • assume that your audience already knows something about sustainability-based next generation assessment, but will benefit from a summary of its key purposes, components and their interactions;
  • recognize that next generation assessment expectations address matters of substance (what should be examined, what analysis should be undertaken) and matters of process (how deliberations should be organized and decisions made) that are all part of the same package;
  • consider the significance, benefits and difficulties of transition from past (and still common) approaches to impact assessment
    • at the project and regional/strategic levels; and
    • for the diversity of assessment authorities, stakeholders and other interested parties (including those in the categories you will be representing in the class engagement discussions);
  • recognize that jurisdictions beyond Canada have more or less different priority concerns, capacities;
  • ensure that, in accord with briefing note style, the main body of your note, is easy to scan and no more than 1000 words, and other matters (specific issues and considerations, case details, important supporting evidence, alternative response options, etc.) are referenced in the main text and presented in a concise appendix or two near the end (just before your bibliography); and
  • see pages 9-11 above for the generic expectations and the general rubric for evaluating the briefing notes, including this one.

Briefing note #2: on sustainability, complexity and cumulative effects in next generation assessment

The second briefing note focuses issues and materials from sessions 4-8 and is meant to consolidate and demonstrate your understanding of how sustainability, complexity and cumulative effects should be addressed in sustainability-based next generation assessment. For this somewhat more ambitious briefing note, the maximum is 2250 words, including appendices but not including references. The paper is due in the dropbox before midnight on Friday, March 15. It represents 30% of the final grade.

Purpose: Briefing note #2 is to illustrate how complexity and cumulative effects considerations should be addressed in the design of a future sustainability-based next generation assessment process for a particular case.

Expectations: The briefing note must

  • cover the issues and materials addressed in sessions 4-8;
  • be addressed to members of a senior planning and assessment advisory body serving the Ontario Government, including representatives of the nine categories of assessment participants identified for our discussions in this course;
  • inform the members’ deliberations on how a sustainability-based next generation assessment process should be used to identify and evaluate an existing proposal for a major new highway project in light of alternative transportation options to facilitate population and employment growth in the GTA/Greenbelt region in Ontario, but also address other sustainability-related imperatives in the region and beyond;
  • draw from the sessions 4-8 course material (and other sources as needed) on how to implement sustainability-based assessment, how to address the realities of complex social and ecological systems and their interactions, how to incorporate respect for uncertainty and precaution in assessments, and how assessments can be helpful in identifying and finding ways to manage cumulative effects;
  • also pay attention to the other considerations in the next generation assessment package and the considerations raised in session 3 concerning planning and assessment issues in the GTA/Greenbelt region in Ontario; and
  • identify and compare major assessment-related options for addressing these matters in the case; and
  • set out and provide justification for recommendations for a preferred approach to be considered by the advisory body.

In drafting in the briefing note, you should

  • assume that the readers of the briefing note will be familiar with the region, and the major issues surrounding facilitation of growth while respecting other considerations and imperatives, and therefore need only basic summaries on that background in the briefing note;
  • assume that the readers of the briefing note have already had a briefing on sustainability-based next generation assessment and will need only a basic recapitulation;
  • consider matters of substance (what issues and options should be examined, what analysis should be undertaken) and matters of process (how deliberations should be organized, who should be involved and how decisions should be made) that are all part of the same package; and
  • adhere to briefing note style expectations, recognizing that the main body of this note, like your previous one, should be no more than 1000 words, leaving the remaining 1250 words for numbered appendices covering specifics (particular issues, options and considerations, case details, supporting evidence, alternative response options, etc.)
    • as in briefing note #1, the appendix material should be concise, easy to scan and clearly flagged in relevant places in the main body of the note; and
  • remember that the generic expectations and the general rubric for evaluating the briefing notes (pages 8-10, above) apply to this briefing note, but with a more generous word count limit.

Briefing note #3: on application of sustainability-based next generation approaches to regional/strategic assessments, linked with project assessments, on matters that should be addressed with effective public engagement and in collaborations with multiple authorities, including Indigenous ones.

The final briefing note is to draw from the materials and learning from the whole course, but focus especially sessions 9-12. In it, you are to incorporate your understanding of how next generation assessment approaches should inform a collaborative multi-jurisdictional agreement for a regional/strategic assessment tiered with project assessments in a region that would benefit from long-term joint planning and management of development and conservation activities. This paper is limited to 2000 words, including appendices but not including the prefatory statement about the region (see below) or the references. It is to be submitted electronically to the course Learn website before midnight on Friday, April 12. It represents 25% of the final grade.

Purpose: Briefing note #3 is to describe application of sustainability-based next generation assessment to arrangements for linked multi-jurisdictional regional/strategic and project level assessments

Context: The year is 2030. You have a UW undergraduate degree, a diploma in assessment and maybe other credentials, but also several years of diverse experience in multiple Canadian jurisdictions, applying next generation assessment principles. And you have been asked to apply for a dream job with a small but highly respected independent Canadian consulting collective.

If you get the job, you will join an elite team assembled to provide impartial, expert advice to several jurisdictions as they negotiate a collaborative next generation assessment agreement. The agreement would authorize and guide a joint regional/strategic assessment of options for long-term joint management of development and conservation activities in a region where proposals for multiple new project-level activities are expected. The joint regional/strategic assessment is meant in part to establish a regional structure of planning and assessment of the individual projects which would ordinarily be subject to more or less separate project-level assessments by the various jurisdictions involved.

You are not the only applicant and along with the usual resumé, transcripts and letters of reference, the team organizer has asked you to provide a briefing note. It is to set out your argument and recommendations on the desirable contents of the multi-jurisdictional collaborative agreement for the linked regional/strategic and project level assessment, as informed by next generation assessment principles.

The job would involve a real 2030 region and collaborative agreement negotiation. The team organizer has provided the specifics. However, since we are stuck in 2024 at the moment, this briefing note will have to be about a hypothetical case. You get to sketch on the essentials of the hypothetical region and suite of issues to address for the purposes of the assignment.

Expectations for the prefatory outline of the case/region: For the purposes of the assignment:

  • you must provide a cover sheet for the briefing note with a short prefatory statement (no more than 200 words, perhaps largely in point form) setting out the essential information about the hypothetical region, relevant jurisdictions, key concerns and opportunities, the kinds of anticipated projects, and major associated issues and options to be considered;
  • the parties negotiating the agreement must include the federal government, at least one province, and a territorial and/or Indigenous government;
  • the region must be in Canada, but cannot be the Southern Ontario region addressed in briefing note #2;
  • for practicality and alignment with actual possibilities, you should consider basing your discussion on a real current region and set of issues, such as
  • the Ring of Fire region, with its remote Indigenous communities, mining development interests, infrastructure needs, and vulnerable boreal ecosystems, etc.; or
  • northeastern British Columbia, with its history of extensive industrial development, hydroelectric dams, and the Blueberry River First Nations cumulative effects law case. etc.

Expectations for the briefing note: In the context summarized above, the briefing note must

  • draw from your learning and the materials from the whole course and the previous diploma courses, while paying particular attention to
    • the full suite of next generation assessment components and associated topics considered in more detail in the course, and
    • the lecture material and readings from sessions 9-12 (along with reliable other sources) on cumulative effects, regional/strategic and tiered assessment, meaningful public processes, and collaborative multi-jurisdictional assessments with Indigenous authorities;
  • be addressed to the organizer of the team identified above;
  • demonstrate your knowledge of sustainability-based next generation assessment and your ability to describe how it would be applied in a practical situation;
  • demonstrate your skills in using the briefing note form;
  • set out your arguments and recommendations on the key desirable contents of the multi-jurisdictional collaborative agreement for the linked regional/strategic and project level assessment, as informed by next generation assessment principles;
  • to the extent possible in a briefing note, incorporate attention to the full suite of next generation assessment components and associated topics considered in more detail the course;
  • recognize that the agreement will have to address both the matters of substance and the matters of process in sustainability-based next generation assessment, including
    • how implementation of the agreement would
      • involve and respect the three (or more) authorities;
      • provide for engagement of other authorities, stakeholders, and public participants;
      • seek and use different forms of knowledge and expertise;
      • identify and assess issues and response options;
    • what the regional/strategic assessment would be expected to deliver to the three (or more) authorities, and what the implications might be for subsequent project planning and assessments and for other activities in the region;
    • how decisions should be made on recommendations and follow-up at both the regional/strategic and project assessment levels;
  • attempt to ensure that the negotiated agreement recognizes that efforts to address these topics affect each other and should be designed to be combined in a package of mutually consistent and mutually supporting components; and
  • adhere (with flexibility) to briefing note style and format
    • with the main body of the note using no more than 1000 words, leaving the remaining 1000 words for numbered appendices covering specifics;
    • ensuring both parts are concise, clearly linked and easy to scan, and are properly referenced.

In drafting the briefing note, you can assume

  • that the team has reasonable familiarity with the region, jurisdictions and case and knows something about sustainability-based next generation assessment, but needs your expertise on treating the next generation components as a package and in setting out the implications for practical application in the negotiations and in the region;
  • that in addition to the participating jurisdictional authorities, diverse stakeholders and other interested parties are aware of the anticipated negotiations and interested in having a voice in the deliberations; and
  • that the agreement would involve both
    • an initial collaborative regional/strategic assessment of options for long-term joint planning and management of development and conservation activities in a region, and
    • subsequent or possibly concurrent project-level assessments

You will not be expected to demonstrate great expertise in the technical aspects or other specifics of the regional context, concerns and opportunities you choose to address. The emphasis is on illustrating how sustainability-based next generation assessment processes should work in some of the most demanding and important applications ­– collaborative multi-authority initiatives linking assessments at the regional and project levels.

Generative AI

The use of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) trained using large language models (LLM) or other methods to produce text, images, music, or code, such as Chat GPT, DALL-E, or GitHub CoPilot, is not permitted in this course. 

Work produced with the assistance of AI tools does not represent the author’s original work and is therefore in violation of the fundamental values of academic integrity including honesty, trust, respect, fairness, responsibility and courage (ICAI, n.d.).

No less importantly, the use GenAI tools conflicts with the purposes of the course. ERS 415 aims to foster the independent development and application of advanced individual capacities that are increasing needed in the world. These include capacities for assessing diverse perspectives, anticipating complex and interactive effects, undertaking informed critical analysis, and designing and pursuing transformative innovations – all of which depend on understanding and foresight. GenAI tools tools have neither. 

The products of ChatGPT and other GenAI tools are often unreliable, for example because the sources used can be out of date, the ability to recognize accurate supporting evidence is weak, and the tool itself has no capacity for critical judgment. More importantly, reliance upon these tools cripples personal development of the needed capacities. 

Unauthorized use in this course, such as running course materials through GenAI or using GenAI to complete a course assessment is considered a violation of Policy 71 (plagiarism or unauthorized aids or assistance). Work produced with the assistance of AI tools does not represent the author’s original work and is therefore in violation of the fundamental values of academic integrity including honesty, trust, respect, fairness, responsibility and courage (ICAI, n.d.).

You should be prepared to show your work. To demonstrate your learning, you should keep your rough notes, including research notes, brainstorming, and drafting notes. You may be asked to submit these notes along with earlier drafts of their work, either through saved drafts or saved versions of a document. If the use of GenAI is suspected where not permitted, you may be asked to meet with your instructor or TA to provide explanations to support the submitted material as being your original work. Through this process, if you have not sufficiently supported your work, academic misconduct allegations may be brought to the Associate Dean.

In addition, you should be aware that the legal/copyright status of generative AI inputs and outputs is unclear. More information is available from the Copyright Advisory Committee.
