Course Description

Calendar Description for PHIL 226

An examination of ethical issues in the health sciences, such as the patient-doctor relationship, reproductive rights and technologies, genetic testing, allocation of medical resources, and end-of-life decisions.

This course includes topics that are morally difficult and sometimes emotionally difficult. It is very likely that when we have discussions we will not all agree. So, we need to think about what respectful disagreement looks like.

Sometimes when two people disagree, they say to each other: “Let’s just agree to disagree. You have your beliefs and I have mine. I don’t need to try to understand you. I won't ask you critical question about your beliefs. I will simply respect your right to think differently than me.”

That view probably has good intentions and it is appropriate in some contexts, but in this class we will take another approach. We will do our best to find respectful ways to ask critical questions during our discussions with each other. The key to doing that is to think about the difference between being adversarial and being constructive. We want to be constructive.

If you are in a debate club, your goal is to defeat your opponent. It is adversarial. Philosophy is not a debate club. Philosophy is based on the idea that one person thinking alone won’t get very far, because each person has limits to their experience, imagination, knowledge, and so on. You can make more progress if other people are willing to sincerely try to understand your view and then ask you honest and challenging questions about the parts they don’t understand or don’t agree with, where their goal is to help you think more carefully and clearly about your view. When someone helps you in this way you might end up having a deeper and clearing understanding of the view you already had or you might end up changing your view -- either result is good. That is being constructive and that is what respectful disagreement looks like.

I should add that respectful disagreement must also be compatible with all of us working together to have an inclusive atmosphere in this class. This requires a few things: (1) Each of us tries our best to express ideas in ways that are not sexist, racist, ableist, queerphobic, etc., knowing that you may sometimes make honest mistakes based on your currently limited knowledge and that is okay, (2) Each of us assumes that other people have positive intent and are open to feedback, and (3) Each of us welcomes feedback from others to help recognize prejudices you may unknowingly have and to help you learn more inclusive ways of thinking and speaking.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course students should be able to:
Read and analyze complicated texts, so that you can: (1) Identify the author’s position; (2) Identify the reasons the author gives for their position, and understand how the reasons are presented to form an argument for the position; (3) Imagine some alternative positions; and (4) Consider the reasons for the alternatives.
Think critically about an ethical argument, so that you can: (1) Ask questions of clarification about an author’s position; (2) Raise potential counter-arguments and offer potential responses to them; and (3) In general, try to understand a variety of perspectives on an issue in order to gain greater clarity about your own thoughts about it.
Express your view about an ethical issue, so that you can: (1) State your position in a way that is precise and detailed, helping your audience understand the ways in which your position is the same as and/or different than the main alternatives; (2) Explain your reasons for your position, helping your audience understand why those reasons support your position rather than one of the alternatives; and (3) Show respect for alternative views by considering and responding to arguments against your view.

Tentative Course Schedule

First, I will describe the overall structure of the course.

We have 24 class meetings. The course is divided into three Units. Unit 1 is six classes, Unit 2 is ten, and Unit 3 is eight.

The purpose of Unit 1 is to give you some examples of ethical reasoning and argumentation so that you can learn about what philosophical arguments look like and how they work. There will be a multiple choice Learn quiz on Unit 1.

Unit 2 has three purposes. First, we spend four classes on the ethics of prenatal testing in order to help us think about bioethical issues related to sex, gender, and disability. Second, we spend two classes on one or two topics that will be based on student votes. Third, we spend three classes learning about some general ethical concepts relevant to bioethics: utilitarianism, care ethics, and relational autonomy. The major assignment for Unit 2 is a philosophical essay. The essay topics for this assignment will be announced in class and on Learn and will give you the opportunity to write about prenatal testing or the topic(s) based on student votes. The readings on utilitarianism, care ethics, and relational autonomy are optional; you can choose whether or not to discuss them in your essay. There are two further assignments in Unit 2 that you must do. They are designed to help you prepare for the essay. One is a multiple choice Learn quiz on the readings from the first four classes of Unit 2. The other is an in-class test that will have questions that guide you into writing a first rough draft of your essay.

Unit 3 likewise has three purposes. First, we spend four classes on the ethics of doctor-patient disagreement and Indigenous health. Second, we spend two classes on one or two topics that will be based on student votes. Third, we spend one class learning about another bioethical concept: justice. Just like in Unit 2, there will be a multiple choice Learn quiz on the readings from the first four classes, an in-class test that guides you into writing a first rough draft of your essay, and an essay assignment.

The test will receive minimal or no comments, and will be assessed using three simple categories: Good, Pass, Fail. "Good" means that your test indicates that you made a good effort to engage with the material and express your ideas about it. My expectation is that most student will receive this. "Pass" means that your tests indicates minimal effort with a significant lack of engagement with the material and/or a serious lack of intelligibility in expressing your ideas. "Fail" means that you either did not write the test or your test indicates an unacceptable level of engagement. If your test is assessed as Good, then it will be temporarily assigned a grade of 70, which will later be changed to be the same as the grade you get on your Essay assignment. A test that is assessed as Pass receives a grade of 50. A test that is assessed as Fail receives a grade of 0.

Instructions for the Essays

  • Length: Your essay must have (1) a title page, (2) four to five pages of the essay itself, and (3) a Works Cited page.
  • Formatting: Times New Roman font, 12 point, double spaced, 1 inch margins.
  • Your essay should have four sections. For more information, make sure to read “Essay Guidelines” on Learn.
    • Introduction (about 75-125 words)
    • Exposition of one or more assigned readings (about 400-500 words)
    • Your Argument (about 400-500 words)
    • Conclusion (about 75-125 words)
  • Please use headings to indicate those four sections.
  • Preferred citation format is Author-Date Chicago Style.
  • It is entirely acceptable to discuss only the relevant course readings and have only them on your Work Cited page. You are allowed, but not expected, to discuss other sources (which you would need to cite, of course).

What you are allowed, and not allowed, to do during the essay writing process in this course

Note: This applies only to this course this term with this instructor.

Generative AI, such as ChatGPT - Yes, conditionally

  • Generative AI, such as Chat GPT is conditionally allowed in this course this term with this instructor. You are not allowed to use GenAI during the In-Class Tests that guide you into writing a first rough draft of your essay, but you are allowed to use GenAI during the process of getting from your first draft to your final version of your Essay. If you wish you use GenAI, then you are required to do two things:
    1. At the end of your Essay, you must attach a copy of every AI output you used for the essay, as well as a copy of your inputs (that is, the words or phrase that you typed into the AI program). These will be like appendices and will not count as part of your word count.
    2. You must treat outputs of AI as if they are published works by a human author. What this means is that if you use any wording from an output, you must put it in quotes and provide a citation; and if you paraphrase or discuss any ideas that you read about in the AI's output, quotes are not necessary but you must still provide a citation. Recommendations for how to cite generative AI in student work at the University of Waterloo may be found through the Library:  https://subjectguides.uwaterlo....
  • Although I am allowing the use of generative AI, I strongly recommend that you do not use AI.
  • Reasons to not use AI:
    1. If you use content written by AI, then you are fully responsible for that content, which means that any mistakes AI makes are now your mistakes. For example, if AI plagiarizes, then this counts as you plagiarizing; if AI makes up a false fact, this counts as you making up a false fact; and so on.
    2. The grading of the final version of your essay will involve an assessment of the quality of exposition and the quality of argument (explained in more detail in the Grading Guidelines document). In terms of exposition, you must explain not only a thinker’s position, but also – and more importantly – the reasons they give for their position. In other words, the thinker’s why is more important than the thinker’s what. The best way to do a good job explaining a thinker’s reasons is to write the exposition yourself based on your own understanding of the assigned readings and course lectures. If you haven’t put in the work yourself to gain your own understanding, then you won’t be able to effectively assess whether AI-generated content does a good job explaining the why; and if you do put in the work, then you’re in a good position to write it yourself. Likewise, in terms of argument, you must explain not only your position, but also – and more importantly – your reasons. Again, your why is more important than your what. The best and easiest way do to a good job explaining your reasons is to write the argument yourself about points that you personally understand.
    3. Writing is part of the thinking process. Every philosopher I have met says this. It is not as if they fully figure out what they think and then simply put it down on paper; instead, they use the writing process to help them figure out what they think. In other words, a good writing process is one that helps you with your thinking process. With that in mind, I personally think that AI would be an obstacle rather than a tool for my writing and thinking process.
  • Reasons to use AI
    1. I personally wouldn’t use AI because of the risk that AI will make mistakes, such as plagiarism, which then count as your mistakes.
    2. All that being said, in this class only I am allowing AI in order to give you an opportunity to try it out if you feel a need to. Maybe using AI in some way can help if you are experiencing writer’s block, or can help with brainstorming, or give you food for thought. I honestly think it would create more problems for you than it might solve, and that you would spend more time overall because of the need to personally check for AI-generated mistakes. Remember that you must properly cite any AI-generated content you use.

Permission from an instructor, TA or the University is also necessary before sharing the intellectual property of others from completed courses with students taking the same/similar courses in subsequent terms/years.  In many cases, instructors might be happy to allow distribution of certain materials. However, doing so without expressed permission is considered a violation of intellectual property rights.

Please alert the instructor if you become aware of intellectual property belonging to others (past or present) circulating, either through the student body or online. The intellectual property rights owner deserves to know (and may have already given their consent).

Academic freedom at the University of Waterloo

Policy 33, Ethical Behaviour states, as one of its general principles (Section 1), “The University supports academic freedom for all members of the University community. Academic freedom carries with it the duty to use that freedom in a manner consistent with the scholarly obligation to base teaching and research on an honest and ethical quest for knowledge. In the context of this policy, 'academic freedom' refers to academic activities, including teaching and scholarship, as is articulated in the principles set out in the Memorandum of Agreement between the FAUW and the University of Waterloo, 1998 (Article 6). The academic environment which fosters free debate may from time to time include the presentation or discussion of unpopular opinions or controversial material. Such material shall be dealt with as openly, respectfully and sensitively as possible.” This definition is repeated in Policies 70 and 71, and in the Memorandum of Agreement, Section 6

University Policy

Academic integrity: In order to maintain a culture of academic integrity, members of the University of Waterloo community are expected to promote honesty, trust, fairness, respect and responsibility. [Check the Office of Academic Integrity for more information.]

Grievance: A student who believes that a decision affecting some aspect of their university life has been unfair or unreasonable may have grounds for initiating a grievance. Read Policy 70, Student Petitions and Grievances, Section 4. When in doubt, please be certain to contact the department’s administrative assistant who will provide further assistance.

Discipline: A student is expected to know what constitutes academic integrity to avoid committing an academic offence, and to take responsibility for their actions. [Check the Office of Academic Integrity for more information.] A student who is unsure whether an action constitutes an offence, or who needs help in learning how to avoid offences (e.g., plagiarism, cheating) or about “rules” for group work/collaboration should seek guidance from the course instructor, academic advisor, or the undergraduate associate dean. For information on categories of offences and types of penalties, students should refer to Policy 71, Student Discipline. For typical penalties, check Guidelines for the Assessment of Penalties.

Appeals: A decision made or penalty imposed under Policy 70, Student Petitions and Grievances (other than a petition) or Policy 71, Student Discipline may be appealed if there is a ground. A student who believes they have a ground for an appeal should refer to Policy 72, Student Appeals.

Note for students with disabilities: AccessAbility Services, located in Needles Hall, Room 1401, collaborates with all academic departments to arrange appropriate accommodations for students with disabilities without compromising the academic integrity of the curriculum. If you require academic accommodations to lessen the impact of your disability, please register with AccessAbility Services at the beginning of each academic term.

Turnitin.com: Text matching software (Turnitin®) may be used to screen assignments in this course. Turnitin® is used to verify that all materials and sources in assignments are documented. Students' submissions are stored on a U.S. server, therefore students must be given an alternative (e.g., scaffolded assignment or annotated bibliography), if they are concerned about their privacy and/or security. Students will be given due notice, in the first week of the term and/or at the time assignment details are provided, about arrangements and alternatives for the use of Turnitin in this course.

It is the responsibility of the student to notify the instructor if they, in the first week of term or at the time assignment details are provided, wish to submit alternate assignment.
